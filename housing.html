Predicting California house prices, and testing every assumption on the way

Four regression models on 20,640 California districts from the 1990 census. The interesting part is not the best score. It is the two cleaning steps that looked sensible, which I tested and found made things worse.

The problem

The task is to predict the median house value of a district from nine features: location, income, housing age, and counts of rooms, bedrooms, people and households. Prices are capped at $500,001 in the data, which affects 4.7% of districts.

Keeping the test data clean

  • I split the data 80/20 before fitting anything.
  • Missing values, scaling and clustering were all fitted on the training set only, then applied to the test set. Fitting them on all the data would leak information and flatter the results.
  • I built three per-household ratio features and checked each against the raw numbers, instead of assuming they helped.

Finding market segments

K-Means clustering on location and income split the market into three groups: southern California, northern California, and a scattered high-income group whose house values were about double the others.

The random forest is the most accurate, but the big gap between its training and test scores shows it memorises a lot. The neural network is two points less accurate with a much smaller gap, so its training score tells you more honestly how it will do on new data.

A debugging story

My first support vector model scored R² 0.43, worse than plain linear regression. Searching more settings barely changed it, which ruled out a bad search. The real cause was scale: the model's tolerance settings work in the units of the target, and house prices are in the hundreds of thousands. Standardising the target lifted R² from 0.43 to 0.78. The neural network needed the same fix.

Two assumptions that turned out wrong

Adding the cluster label did not help. It changed test R² by 0.0015 at most. The clusters were built from features the models could already see, so the label added nothing new.
Removing the price-capped districts made every model worse. The random forest dropped from 0.824 to 0.801. Those districts are mostly truly expensive areas, so deleting them removes real signal.

What the numbers mean in practice

The best model is off by about $32,000 on a typical district, roughly 18%. That is good enough for screening or comparing areas, and not good enough to value a single home. The data is also from 1990, so the model describes that market, not today's.
